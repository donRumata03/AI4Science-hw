## 1. Introduction - pp.2-3

The motivation is to reduce both sequential generation steps and the cost of each
step. NCM changes the representation and generation order: short coarse code
sequences precede longer refinements. The authors compare quality, diversity,
sampling time and training exposure; speed alone is not the entire claim.

## 2. Related work - pp.3-4

NCM combines residual quantization and next-scale prediction for text. Its learned
discrete residual levels differ from continuous text latents, semantic vocabulary
clusters and sentence-level concepts. The word concept describes information in
coarse levels; it does not establish an interpretable semantic unit for each code.

## 3.1. Quantization background - pp.5-6

Nearest-codebook lookup discretizes latent vectors. Residual quantization adds
successive corrections. The multiscale version maps each shorter code level back
to a full-length contribution before adding it to the cumulative reconstruction.

## 3.2. Generative formulation - p.6, Equations 3-5

Level lengths increase to the text length. The model factors across levels using
the earlier cumulative reconstruction as condition, and also factors across
positions inside the current level. The latter enables one-pass sampling. It is
a modeling restriction; the paper does not prove that the true code distribution
is conditionally independent under that reconstruction.

## 3.3. Tokenizer - pp.6-7, Equations 6-9

An encoder produces text latents. Each level downsamples the residual, quantizes
it and contributes a reconstruction. Nested dropout samples a retained prefix
and evaluates reconstruction/VQ losses at that cutoff. This encourages useful
early reconstructions. It does not by itself optimize the ease of their prior.

## 3.4. Generator - p.7, Equation 10 and Algorithms 1-2

The tokenizer is frozen for generator training. Earlier-level reconstructions are
resized/projected into inputs; all training levels can be predicted in one pass.
Generation instead visits levels sequentially, samples a level in parallel and
updates the reconstruction. Conditioning codes may be corrupted in training while
target labels remain clean. Prompt-conditioned use is supported through prefix-LM attention.

## 4.1. Tokenizer evaluation - p.8, Table 1

The authors measure exact token reconstruction and codebook utilization. The
reported reconstruction is dataset-specific: OWT 99.99%, LM1B 99.86%, with other
values for other tasks. A nearly lossless reconstruction is neither a guarantee
of coherent generated codes nor proof that learned code priors are sufficient.

## 4.2. Unconditional generation - p.9, Figure 5

GenPPL uses GPT-2-large as reference scorer and is accompanied by mean per-sample
unigram entropy. Their trade-off matters because concentrating generation can
lower GenPPL. These measurements do not substitute for the proposed corpus-level
diversity and MAUVE study under a shared preprocessing protocol.

## 4.3-4.4. Conditional tasks and reasoning - pp.9-10

Translation uses BLEU, summarization uses ROUGE, and Sudoku uses solved-board
accuracy. Results concern the reported datasets and decoding settings. They do
not establish improvement from a within-level denoising adaptation that is absent
from the baseline architecture.

## 4.5-4.6. Scaling and cost - pp.11-12

The paper studies model size, multiple-candidate MBR selection, end-to-end sampling
time and training-token exposure. NFE alone omits unequal per-call costs. A future
adaptation must report actual denoiser calls and wall time, with matched controls.

## 4.7 and 5. Samples and conclusion - pp.12-13

Examples illustrate the trajectory rather than prove semantic decomposition or
general reasoning ability. The conclusion attributes gains to learned coarse-to-fine
representations and parallel level prediction. Our research isolates the effect of
replacing the within-level factorization while retaining the tokenizer and outer order.
